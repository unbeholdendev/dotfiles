---
name: natural-voice
description: Voice for prose people read, so it sounds like a person wrote it. Use when writing or editing a blog post, article or site copy, or when asked to make text sound natural or less AI-generated.
---

1. Read two or three published pieces from the same place, and write in their voice.
2. Write or edit. Keep every fact, link and section the author gave; change the wording.
3. Hunt the tells below, sentence by sentence, and rewrite each one you find. Done when a full
   pass from the top finds none.
4. List the real details that would make it better and that only the author knows, and ask for
   them. A made-up detail is worse than a missing one.
5. Report under three headings: **What gave it away** (the tells, quoted), **Changes**, and
   **Worth adding yourself** (the list from step 4).

## Voice

Contractions. Plain words. Sentences of mixed length, most of them medium. British spelling. A
comma, full stop or brackets where an em-dash would go. Headings that say what the section holds.
The piece ends on its last real point.

## Tells

- No contractions: "I have", "does not", "it is".
- "X, not Y", "X rather than Y" and "X and not Y", said again and again.
- A short punchy line ending a paragraph or section: "The blast radius is total.", "Read it
  before you put money in."
- A tidy last line that loops back to the title or flips a phrase on itself.
- Signposting: "This post is about", "let me explain", "which we'll get to", "Here's the thing".
- Definitions dropped in as a line of their own: "Financial sovereignty is the opposite:".
- Tools and ideas that act like people: "Secrets want care", "Headscale is doing a lot of quiet
  work", "What the shape buys".
- Lists of three for rhythm: "No bank, no provider, and nobody in the loop".
- Bold-lead bullets where plain bullets or prose would do.
- The same point made twice in different words.
- Stock reassurance: "You don't have to work it out alone".
- A claim the author never made, like how long something takes.
